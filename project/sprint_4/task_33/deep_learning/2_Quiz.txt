Quiz

1. What was the intern's initial problem and proposed
   solution regarding the validatePersonName and validateDate methods?

2. According to the mentor, what are the two main
   approaches to implementing business requirements in code?

3. What is the primary drawback of the "optimization"
   approach (removing duplicate methods) from a business perspective?

4. What is a key advantage of the approach where each
   business requirement is implemented separately,
   even if it leads to code duplication?

5. Why does the mentor suggest that an overly
   optimized technical solution can become "hell to support"
   in complex systems?

6. What does the mentor mean by stating that "clean
   code and all empirical rules of writing code are not axioms"?

7. How does the mentor define the "true art of programming"?

8. What crucial step does the mentor advise developers
   to take when faced with a problem, beyond just
   implementing one solution?

9. Why does the mentor emphasize that university
   education often fails to prepare students for
   real-world development challenges like the one discussed?

10. What advice does the mentor give regarding situations
    where a developer cannot see alternative solutions?


Quiz Answer Key

1. The intern noticed two sets of almost identical
   validation methods (for names and dates) and consolidated
   them into two generic validatePersonName and validateDate
   methods to avoid code duplication, believing it was a good
   optimization.

2. The two main approaches are: optimizing to remove repeating
   methods, or implementing each business requirement separately,
   even if it means some code duplication.

3. The primary drawback is that it introduces technical
   dependencies between business requirements in the code,
   which do not exist in the business logic, making future
   changes more complex as one change might affect unrelated
   requirements.

4. A key advantage of implementing each business requirement
   separately is that the technical solution very closely
   mirrors the business requirements, making it much easier
   to identify and modify the code for a specific business
   change without impacting other requirements.

5. An overly optimized technical solution becomes "hell to support"
   because it diverges significantly from the business solution.
   This makes it incredibly difficult to trace which specific
   piece of code implements a particular business requirement,
   leading to confusion and maintenance nightmares.

6. The mentor means that while "clean code" principles offer
   valuable guidance, they are not absolute truths to be
   followed rigidly. Every solution has trade-offs, and the
   best approach depends on the specific context rather than
   a universal rule.

7. The mentor defines the true art of programming as finding
   the optimal balance between creating a technically robust
   and "cool" solution and ensuring that the technical
   implementation remains as close as possible to the
   business process it automates.

8. The mentor advises developers to always seek out and
   evaluate alternative solutions. By comparing the pros
   and cons of multiple approaches, a developer can make
   a more informed decision about which solution is best
   for the given context.

9. University education often fails because it uses oversimplified,
   "detached from reality" examples. These examples don't present
   the complex trade-offs and nuanced decisions that are common
   in real-world software development, thus leaving students
   unprepared.

10. The mentor advises that if a developer cannot see alternatives,
    it means they lack sufficient knowledge, experience, or context.
    In such situations, it is crucial to seek help from others
    (collaborate) to find better solutions and expand one's
    perspective.
